Recipe 43: Exams students can prepare for

FormativeSaves time

One page, for the end of the term, when exams are close. It builds on Recipe 1 (rubrics), Recipe 3 (low-stakes quizzes) and Recipe 4 (multiple-choice questions), and leads to Recipe 8 (exams that measure the outcomes).

The problem

The week before an exam, the email starts: "What's on it?" "Will there be a review?" "Can we have a cheat sheet?" After it come the regrade requests and the students who did the reading and still failed. Both have the same two causes.

First, the exam tests what was easy to write and grade, not what the course set out to teach. Second, students prepare by rereading their notes, which feels like studying but is one of the weakest ways to learn. Nothing in the course shows them a better way, so they don't use one.

What it looks like

A one-page exam blueprint: each outcome the exam covers, how much it counts, and the kind of question that tests it. You write it before any question, and students get it two weeks before the exam.

Preparing for the exam is part of the course, not left to students. They answer practice questions from memory over several weeks, see questions that combine topics before the exam does, and get a practice exam with its key. The answer key and rubric are finished before the exam is given. Afterward, a short exam wrapper asks students how they studied and what they will change.

Why it works

The exam matches the course. Outcomes, teaching and assessment should line up, which is constructive alignment (Biggs 1996). A blueprint is the simplest way to check that they do, and to show students what counts (Winkelmes et al. 2016).

Recall beats rereading. Students who practice recalling material remember far more a week later than students who reread it, even with no feedback on their answers (Roediger and Karpicke 2006; Yang et al. 2021). Spreading that practice over weeks makes it last (Cepeda et al. 2006).

Mixed practice teaches the choice. A question that combines topics tests whether students can pick the right method. Practicing one topic at a time never asks them to; mixed practice does (Rohrer and Taylor 2007).

A rubric written first grades more fairly. Rubrics set in advance make scores more consistent across graders and papers (Jonsson and Svingby 2007), and writing the key is when you catch a question that does not work.

The smallest version

About 30 minutes, once, for any size of course, and no extra grading.

  1. Post a blueprint and an old exam. Write one paragraph: the topics on the exam and how much each counts. Post it with last term's exam and its key, or a short practice set if there is no old exam. About 20 minutes.
  2. Ten minutes of one class: a concept map from memory. Students close their notes and draw the unit's main ideas and how they connect. Then they compare with a neighbor, open their notes and fill the gaps. You grade nothing.
  3. A five-question wrapper after the exam. Run it as a Canvas survey for completion credit. Read the summary, and post one announcement on what to do differently next time.

All three carry over to next term with a date change. What it saves: most of the "what's on the exam?" email, and students who studied hard the wrong way. The steps below are the full version.

What we tend to believe

Five beliefs come up whenever faculty talk about exams. Each is reasonable, and the research points another way.

"I write the rubric while I grade." Grading the first few papers does show what students did. But a rubric set before grading makes scores more consistent across graders and across the stack (Jonsson and Svingby 2007). Shared with students in advance, it also improves the work (Panadero and Jonsson 2013). Write the key and rubric before the exam, and keep a short "other" note for good answers you did not expect. Recipe 1 shows how.

"I teach the topics separately, and the exam puts them together." A question that combines topics tests a skill students have never practiced: choosing which method fits. Mixed practice builds that skill (Rohrer and Taylor 2007). If the exam combines topics, the homework and the practice exam should too, and the blueprint should say so.

"A cheat sheet helps students prepare." It helps less than it seems. Letting students bring a crib card did not improve their scores (Dickson and Miller 2005), and students can come to lean on it (Funk and Dickson 2011). Copying notes onto a card asks them to recall nothing. If you allow one, allow it to ease nerves, not as the study plan.

"Concept maps are a good study tool." They are, with one condition. (New to concept maps? What a concept map is shows one drawn two ways, for a web programming course and a systems course.) Building a concept map beats reading or listening to a lecture (Nesbit and Adesope 2006). But students who practiced recall did better than students who built maps with the text open (Karpicke and Blunt 2011). A map drawn from memory, then checked, works about as well as a practice test (Blunt and Karpicke 2014). Retrieval is what does the work.

"Formative or summative, an exam is an exam." The difference is who acts on the result, and when. Assessment is formative when you and the students use what it shows while there is still time to change something (Black and Wiliam 1998). A practice exam is formative; the final is summative. A course that has only the second gives students nothing to adjust. Recipe 11 goes further.

The steps

  1. Write the blueprint first. List the outcomes the exam covers. Give each a weight and the kind of question that tests it: recall, apply, combine, explain. Questions come after the blueprint, not before.
  2. Write the questions from the blueprint. Every question points to a row. A row with no question is an outcome the exam skips; a question with no row tests something you did not teach.
  3. Write the key and rubric now. Partial credit, the common wrong answers and what each earns. If you cannot write the key for a question in a few minutes, rewrite the question.
  4. Have a TA take it. A common rule of thumb: students need about three times as long as a TA does. Cut questions until the TA finishes in a third of the exam time.
  5. Give students the blueprint and a practice exam about two weeks out. Include at least one question that combines topics, with its worked solution.
  6. Build in retrieval practice. Two or three low-stakes quizzes in the weeks before, mixing old topics with new, auto-graded in Canvas (Recipe 3). Ten minutes of class for a concept map from memory.
  7. Give a wrapper afterward. Five questions: how they studied, how many hours, which mistakes were careless and which were not understanding, and what they will do differently. Post a short summary of what you learned from the answers.

The prompt

Use it twice: once to check the blueprint against your questions, once to draft practice questions. Write the real exam yourself.

You are helping a computer science instructor prepare an exam.

Course outcomes this exam covers, with weights:
[list each outcome and its percent]

Draft exam questions:
[paste them, numbered]

1. Make a table: each question, the outcome it tests, and the kind of
   question (recall, apply, combine topics, explain). Flag any outcome
   whose share of points is more than 5 points off its weight, and any
   question that matches no outcome.
2. Flag any question whose wording could be read two ways.
3. Write 8 practice questions in the same style, not copies of these:
   at least 2 that combine topics, each with a worked solution and the
   most common wrong answer.

Time and cost

For a 200-student course, the extra work beyond writing the exam you would write anyway:

Part Your time Per student Next term
Blueprint 30 minutes none reuse, small edits
Key and rubric before the exam moved earlier, not added none reuse
TA test run 1 TA hour none reuse
Practice exam and key 1 hour, or 10 minutes to post an old one none reuse
Two or three retrieval quizzes 1 hour to write, auto-graded none reuse
Concept map in class 10 minutes of class none reuse
Wrapper and summary 20 minutes none reuse

About four hours the first time, and well under one after that. The AI prompt cuts the practice questions to checking and editing. Cost: none beyond a tool the university already supports.

What students see

Two weeks out, a blueprint that says what the exam covers and how much each part counts, a practice exam with a worked key, and a note on how to study: answer questions from memory, then check, rather than reread. Before the exam, they have already seen a question that combines topics. After it, a few questions about how they studied, and an announcement with what the class as a whole should do differently next time.

What can go wrong

  • The practice exam becomes the exam. Students memorize its answers. Write practice questions in the same style but on different cases, and say so.
  • The blueprint is too vague to help. "Chapters 4 to 7" tells students nothing. Name the outcomes, the weights and the kinds of questions.
  • Nobody does the quizzes. A small amount of credit for completion, not correctness, is enough to get most students to try.
  • AI questions with wrong keys. Work every AI-drafted practice question yourself before posting it.
  • The wrapper goes unread. Read the summary, not every answer, and post one announcement. Students will skip the next wrapper if nothing came of the last one.

Try it this week

Take your next exam. Write the one-paragraph blueprint, post it with an old exam, and run the prompt on your draft questions to see which outcomes they miss.
