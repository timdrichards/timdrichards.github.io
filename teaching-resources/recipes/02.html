Recipe 2: Faster feedback with AI drafts and a person in the loop

Where AI helps
Drafts feedback against the rubric; a person reviews every one; students check it too, and earn credit for evidence that it is wrong
The idea behind it
Feedback. Good feedback answers where am I going, how am I doing, where to next (Hattie and Timperley 2007)

FeedbackSaves timeMore student contact

One page, for the second Teaching Recipes session. It builds on Recipe 1, rubrics that grade quickly and fairly.

The problem

Feedback on written work or code is slow. By the time students get it, they have moved on, and grading takes a large share of TA hours.

What it looks like

An AI drafts feedback on each submission against your rubric. A person (you or a TA) reads every draft, fixes what is wrong, and releases it. Students are told AI helps draft feedback and are invited to flag anything that looks wrong.

In CICS, one colleague uses this in a graduate and an undergraduate course. Students are told, and asked to check the feedback. No student has reported a wrong score or comment, and the instructor, who reviewed every result, found only a few minor issues.

Why it works

Feedback on the work is one of the strongest influences on learning that education research has found (Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie 2020). Hattie and Timperley (2007) put good feedback as answers to three questions a student has: Where am I going? (the goal), How am I doing? (against it) and Where to next? (what to do now). The prompt below asks for all three: the score on each criterion, the line of work that shows it, and one next step. Feedback also helps most while students can still act on it, which is the core of formative assessment (Black and Wiliam 1998). Speed is the point of this recipe.

The smallest version

About 20 minutes per assignment, for any size of course, and no comments to write for each student. Grade with scores as you do now, then give the whole class one round of feedback.

  1. After grading, take 20 to 30 submissions with names removed, and paste them, the assignment and the rubric into the second prompt below.
  2. Read the AI's list of the three most common problems in 10 minutes. Check each example against a real submission, and fix or drop any that do not hold.
  3. Post it as one announcement, or spend five minutes of the next class on it: each problem, an example, and what to do instead.

The same prompt works for every assignment, term after term. What it saves: the same comment written fifty times, and the email that follows a score with no explanation. It also reaches students while they can still use it, which is the point of this recipe (see "Why it works"). The steps below are the full version, with feedback for each student.

The steps

  1. Write the rubric down. Each criterion with what full, partial and no credit look like (Recipe 1). The AI is only as good as this.
  2. Remove names. Strip student names and IDs from the submissions before they go to the AI.
  3. Draft. Give the AI the assignment, the rubric and one submission at a time, using the prompt below.
  4. Review every draft. Read it against the submission. Fix scores, cut anything vague, add one line only a person would say.
  5. Release and tell students what AI did and that they can ask for a human review.
  6. Spot-check yourself. Each week, re-grade a few submissions from scratch and compare.

The prompt

You are helping an instructor draft feedback. Use only the rubric below.
For each criterion: give the score, quote the line of the submission that
justifies it, and write one specific next step the student can act on.
If you are not sure, say so instead of guessing. Do not invent errors.

Assignment: [paste]
Rubric: [paste]
Submission: [paste, with names removed]

For the smallest version, use this prompt instead:

You are helping an instructor give feedback to a whole class.
Below are an assignment, its rubric, and a sample of graded
submissions with names removed. List the three most common problems,
most frequent first. For each: name the rubric criterion, quote a short
example from one submission, and say in one sentence what to do instead.
Do not invent problems that are not in the submissions.

Assignment: [paste]
Rubric: [paste]
Submissions: [paste 20 to 30, names removed]

Time and cost

Time is hard to give as a number, so think of it as a fraction, as in Recipe 1.

  • T is the time to grade one submission from scratch. T grows with the assignment: longer work, more criteria, more ways to be partly right.
  • With an AI draft, reviewing one submission takes about T / P. P is how much the rubric and prompt tell the AI. A rubric built with Recipe 1, with levels a grader can see in the work, raises P.
  • P is hard to measure, but the direction is clear: the more detail, the less there is to fix. Review never drops to zero, though, because a person still reads every submission. A vague rubric (P close to 1) saves little.
  • Setup: testing the prompt on five submissions you have already graded takes about 5T, once. Track review time for the first two weeks and compare it with T.
  • Tool: the college-provided AI tool. Use a university-approved tool for student work.

What students see

A line in the syllabus and on each assignment, for example: "An AI drafts first-pass feedback against the rubric. A member of the teaching staff reviews every comment and score before you see it. If anything looks wrong, ask for a human review and we will regrade it."

Going further: students check the feedback

Inviting students to flag errors can become part of the course. Checking AI output with evidence, rather than accepting or rejecting it, is one of the skills employers ask for most. Education research calls it evaluative judgement, the ability to tell good work from poor, and argues that assessment should build it for AI output as well as for students' own work (Bearman et al. 2024). So give credit for it: a student who comes to office hours with evidence that a comment or score is wrong (the line of code, the spec, the test that passes) gets the points back and a small bonus. The student reads the feedback closely, which is the point of feedback, and the conversation in office hours is feedback in both directions (Recipe 10, regrades as dialogue). It also catches the errors a reviewer missed. Recipe 27 goes further, with students giving themselves feedback, and Recipe 35 collects other reasons to come to office hours.

Where the saved time goes

The less time spent writing comments from scratch, the more there is for students. Put the hours saved into office hours, especially the conversations above. Recipe 33 shows how to plan it.

What can go wrong

  • People stop checking. In a study at IIT Kanpur, some TAs over-relied on AI drafts and passed on the errors (Ahmed et al. 2025). Review has to be real: read the submission, not just the draft.
  • Wrong or vague comments. The prompt asks for a quoted line for every score; drop any comment without one.
  • Faster is not the same as better. In that study, students rated AI-assisted feedback higher, but their scores did not improve. Feedback helps when students act on it, so give them a reason to (a revision, or the next assignment building on this one).

Try it this week

Take one assignment you have already graded. Run ten submissions through the prompt, compare the drafts with your own feedback, and note where they differ. That tells you whether the rubric is clear enough to use this way.

Evidence

  • Ahmed, Sahai, Leong and Karkare, SIGCSE TS 2025: AI drafts for TAs in CS1 at IIT Kanpur; higher student ratings, no score gain, some TA over-reliance. doi:10.1145/3641554.3701972
  • Woodrow, Malik and Piech, SIGCSE TS 2024: instant AI feedback on code style for 8,310 CS1 students; 97% of raters found it helpful. doi:10.1145/3626252.3630773
  • Meyer et al., 2024: AI feedback on essay drafts improved revisions and motivation for 459 secondary students. doi:10.1016/j.caeai.2023.100199
  • Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie, 2020: detailed feedback on the task and how to improve has a large effect on learning (d = 0.99); rewards alone, little (d = 0.24). doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087
  • Bearman, Tai, Dawson, Boud and Ajjawi, 2024: assessment should develop students' evaluative judgement of AI output and processes, not only their own work. doi:10.1080/02602938.2024.2335321
