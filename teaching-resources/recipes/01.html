Recipe 1: Rubrics that grade quickly and fairly

Where AI helps
Drafts a rubric from the spec and outcomes; you edit it
The idea behind it
Clear criteria make grading reliable and show students what good work looks like (Jonsson and Svingby 2007)

One page, for the first Teaching Recipes session. Recipe 2, faster feedback with AI drafts, builds on it.

The problem

Grading a big assignment takes hours, and two graders often give the same work different scores. Students then argue points instead of reading comments. A vague rubric is usually the cause: criteria like "good code quality" leave each grader to decide what good means, and leave students to guess.

What it looks like

A short rubric with 3 to 5 criteria and 3 or 4 levels each. Every level says what a grader can see in the work ("every function has a test, and the tests pass") rather than a judgment ("well tested"). Students get the rubric with the assignment, not after it. Graders score the same few submissions first and fix any criterion they disagree on before grading the rest.

Why it works

Rubrics make grading more consistent when they are analytic (one row per criterion), specific to the task, and used with examples and a short calibration between graders (Jonsson and Svingby 2007). They also help students learn when students see them before the work, because the rubric tells them what the goal is (Panadero and Jonsson 2013; Reddy and Andrade 2010).

This is feedback before the work is even done. Hattie and Timperley's first question for good feedback is "Where am I going?" (2007), and a clear rubric answers it. It is also the idea behind transparent assignment design (TILT Higher Ed): students do better when the purpose, the task and the criteria are spelled out. A rubric written this way also makes Recipe 2 possible, since each level's wording becomes the comment a student reads.

The smallest version

About 30 minutes, once, for any size of course, and no extra grading.

  1. Pick the next assignment you will grade. Paste its spec and its outcomes into the prompt below.
  2. Spend 15 minutes on the draft: cut it to five rows or fewer, and rewrite any level you could not score in under a minute.
  3. Post it with the assignment. If you have TAs, spend 10 minutes of the next staff meeting scoring the same two old submissions with it and fixing the row you disagree on.

Keep it for next term; it needs only small edits. What it saves: fewer "why did I lose points?" arguments, and graders who agree without a long calibration. The steps below are the full version.

The steps

  1. Start from the outcomes. List the 3 to 5 things the assignment is meant to show. Each becomes one criterion. If a criterion does not match an outcome, cut it.
  2. Ask AI for a first draft. Give it the assignment spec and the outcomes, using the prompt below. Ask for observable levels, not adjectives.
  3. Edit it as the expert. Cut criteria that overlap, fix point values, and rewrite any level you could not score in under a minute.
  4. Test it on old work. Score 3 to 5 past submissions, with names removed. Where you hesitate, the wording needs work.
  5. Calibrate with your graders. Each grader scores the same 3 submissions alone. Compare. Rewrite any criterion where scores differ by more than one level.
  6. Give it to students with the assignment. Point to it in class, and use its wording in your comments when you grade.

The prompt

You are helping a computer science instructor write a grading rubric.

Assignment spec:
[paste the spec]

Learning outcomes this assignment should show:
[list 3 to 5 outcomes]

Write an analytic rubric with one row per outcome and 4 levels
(Exceeds, Meets, Approaching, Missing). For each level, describe what a
grader can see in the submission, in one sentence. Avoid words like
"good", "clear" or "strong" unless you say what they look like.
Suggest a point value per row so the total is [N].
Then list two places where graders are most likely to disagree.

Time and cost

Time is hard to give as a number, so think of it as a fraction.

  • T is the time to write the rubric by hand. T grows with the assignment: more parts, more outcomes, more ways to be partly right.
  • With an AI first draft, the work takes about T / P. P is how much the prompt tells the AI: the spec, the outcomes, the point total, and an example of what "Meets" looks like.
  • P is hard to measure, but the direction is clear: the more detail in the prompt, the less there is to fix. A thin prompt (P close to 1) saves little, because you rewrite most of what comes back.
  • Calibration with graders takes about 30 minutes, once per assignment, whatever P is.
  • Cost: none beyond a tool the university already supports.

What students see

The rubric, with the assignment, before they start. When grading comes back, each score points to a row and a level they have already read, so the comment explains the score and says what the next level up looks like. That is feedback they can use on the next assignment.

What can go wrong

  • AI writes generic rubrics. Without your outcomes it falls back on "clarity" and "organization". Always give it the outcomes, and always edit.
  • Too many criteria. Ten rows take longer to grade and blur what matters. Keep it to five or fewer.
  • Grading to the rubric only. A rubric can miss good work it did not expect. Keep a short "other" note for graders to flag it.
  • Student work in AI tools. Remove names before pasting any past work, and use a tool the university supports.

Try it this week

Pick one assignment that is due in the next month. Run the prompt on its spec, edit the result for 20 minutes, and score two old submissions with it. If you have TAs, have one score the same two and compare.
