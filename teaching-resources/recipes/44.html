Recipe 44: A syllabus that runs the semester for you

Saves time

One page, for the weeks before a new term, when next term's syllabus gets written. It pairs with Recipe 43 (exams) and leads to Recipe 15 (outcomes measured two ways) and Recipe 16 (AI rules students read).

The problem

A syllabus written the week before classes leaves the hard decisions for later, and later means email. Can I have an extension? Does this absence count? When is the regrade deadline? Can I use AI on this? Each answer takes a few minutes and a judgment call, and in a 200-student course they arrive every day.

The answers also drift. A student who asks gets a different deal from one who doesn't, and in a course with several sections, students in one section can be graded in a very different way from students in another. That is unfair, and it is how complaints reach the chair.

What it looks like

The syllabus makes the term's decisions once, in advance, so you rarely make them again by email. It has four parts.

  • Three to five outcomes, each tied to the work that shows it, so the grade weights follow from what the course is for.
  • A calendar built backward from the dates that cannot move: the final exam slot, holidays, add/drop, the grade deadline. Every due date leaves time to grade and return the work before the next one builds on it.
  • Policies that decide for you: a bank of late days instead of extension requests, a regrade window, absences that need no doctor's note, and an AI level for each kind of work.
  • A one-page summary at the top with what students look up most: how the grade is made, the late policy, how to reach the staff and where things are posted.

In a course with more than one section, it is one syllabus for every section: the same weights, policies and grading scale.

Why it works

Clear rules help the students who most need them. Students do better when the purpose, the task and the criteria are spelled out, and the gains are largest for first-generation and underserved students (Winkelmes et al. 2016). The UMass Faculty Senate makes the same point: it calls a clear syllabus "an equity issue" and lists 14 required elements (Sen. Doc. 24-066).

The grade follows from the outcomes. Weights that come from what the course is meant to teach line up assessment with learning (Biggs 1996), and they are easier to defend when a student asks.

A learning-focused syllabus reads differently. A syllabus that explains why, invites questions and sets out a schedule of learning, not only rules, can be scored with a published rubric (Palmer, Bach and Streifer 2014). Tone matters too: students rated the instructor of a warmly worded syllabus as more approachable and more motivated than the same course written coldly (Harnish and Bridges 2011).

A rule decided once is applied the same way. Every policy settled in the syllabus is one fewer judgment call made under pressure, one student at a time.

The smallest version

About 30 minutes, once, on last term's syllabus. No new work per student.

  1. Check it against the Senate's list. Paste last term's syllabus into the first prompt below. It lists which of the 14 required elements are missing. Fix those. About 15 minutes.
  2. Pre-decide the three questions you get most. Look at last term's email and pick the three you answered most often, usually extensions, absences and regrades. Write one rule for each into the syllabus: a late-day bank, absences without notes up to a number, a one-week regrade window.
  3. Add a summary box at the top: how the grade is made, the late rule, how to reach staff, where things are posted.

It carries to next term with new dates. What it saves: most of the policy email, and the risk of treating two students differently. The steps below are the full version.

What we tend to believe

"Nobody reads the syllabus anyway." Many don't, which is a reason to make it easy to read, not a reason to skip it. Put the one-page summary first, and give a short syllabus quiz in week one: an old, cheap way to get students to open it (Raymark and Connor-Greene 2002). Then answer policy email with a link to the line.

"A strict syllabus prevents problems." Rules students cannot meet become exceptions you have to grant, one by one. A late-day bank or a dropped lowest score gives the same flexibility to everyone without a single request. It is stricter, in that the rule really holds, and kinder at the same time.

"My section is my course." In a course several people teach, each section's choices are a student's luck of the draw. The same weights, the same policies and, where possible, the same assessments across sections mean a grade means the same thing whoever taught it.

"The syllabus is paperwork." It is the first piece of teaching students see. A syllabus that says why each part of the grade is there, and how to get help, sets the tone of the course before the first lecture (Harnish and Bridges 2011).

The steps

  1. Start from three to five outcomes. For each, name the work that shows it. Set the grade weights from that list, so no single kind of work, such as two exams, carries most of the grade unless the outcomes call for it.
  2. Build the calendar backward. Put in the fixed dates first: the final exam slot, holidays, add/drop, the grade deadline. Then the big deadlines, each with time to grade and return the work before the next one builds on it. Then the weekly rhythm.
  3. Pre-decide the policies. For each, one rule that needs no request: late work (a bank of late days, or a drop), absences (a number that needs no note, then the campus process), regrades (a window and a form), AI use (a level for each kind of work, Recipe 16).
  4. Check the 14 required elements in Sen. Doc. 24-066: number and title, credits and contact hours, staff and contact, office hours, description, learning objectives, delivery mode, materials, requirements and weights, grading scale, schedule, attendance, late work, and the three required statements (accommodations, academic honesty, Title IX). Link the Senate's statements page rather than retyping it.
  5. Share it across sections. If others teach the course, agree on one syllabus before the term: the same weights, policies and scale. Differences in teaching style are fine; differences in how students are graded are not.
  6. Put a summary on page one, and give a five-question syllabus quiz in week one, auto-graded in Canvas.
  7. Keep a list of the questions that still come in. Each one is a rule for next term's syllabus.

The prompt

Two prompts: one checks a draft, one drafts the calendar. Check every date it gives against the registrar's calendar.

You are helping a university instructor check a course syllabus.

Syllabus:
[paste it]

1. Check it against these 14 required elements and say, for each,
   Present, Partial or Missing, quoting the line that shows it:
   course number and title; credits and weekly contact hours; instructor
   and TA names and contact; office hours or appointments; course
   description; learning objectives; delivery mode; course materials;
   requirements and grade weights; grading scale; course schedule;
   attendance policy; late or make-up work policy; statements on
   accommodations, academic honesty and Title IX.
2. List every place a student would have to email the instructor to
   find out what happens (an extension, an absence, a regrade, AI use),
   and suggest one rule for each that needs no request.
3. Say whether any one component carries more than 40% of the grade.
Draft a week-by-week calendar for a [N]-week course.
Fixed dates: [first and last class, holidays, add/drop, final exam slot,
grade deadline]. Major deliverables: [list]. Grading takes [N] days.
Place each deliverable so it is graded and returned before the next one
that builds on it, and flag any week with two major deadlines.

Time and cost

Starting from last term's syllabus, for a course of any size:

Part Your time Next term
Check against the 14 elements 15 minutes with the prompt 5 minutes
Outcomes and weights 30 minutes reuse
Calendar built backward 30 minutes with the prompt 15 minutes, new dates
Pre-decided policies 30 minutes reuse
Summary box and syllabus quiz 30 minutes reuse
Agreeing across sections one 1-hour meeting reuse, a short check

About three hours the first time, under one after that. The return is in email: a 200-student course where each policy question takes three minutes, a few a day, spends hours a term on answers a syllabus line could give. Cost: none.

What students see

A first page that answers what they would otherwise email about: how the grade is made, what happens if they are late or sick, how to reach the staff. Rules that apply to everyone the same way, with no need to ask. A calendar where work comes back before the next piece is due. In a shared course, the same deal as their friends in the other section.

What can go wrong

  • The syllabus gets long. Every rule added makes it harder to read. Keep the summary to one page and move detail to linked pages.
  • Attendance rules stricter than campus policy. The Senate says course policies should not be more stringent than the university's. Check before setting a cap.
  • The calendar is wrong. AI-drafted dates can miss a holiday or a Monday schedule on a Tuesday. Check every date against the registrar's calendar.
  • Late days that pile up. Students save them all for the last week. Cap how many can go on one assignment, or close the bank before the final project.
  • A shared syllabus nobody owns. In a course several people teach, name one person who keeps the syllabus each term.

Try it this week

Open last term's syllabus and run the first prompt on it. Fix whatever is missing, and write one rule for the question you answered most often by email.
