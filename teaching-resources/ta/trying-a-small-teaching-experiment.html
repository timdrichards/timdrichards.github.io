Trying a Small Teaching Experiment

You notice something that isn't working: office hours turn into you debugging students' code, regrade requests pile up after every assignment, nobody answers in section. You have an idea for a fix. A small teaching experiment means trying that one change on purpose and checking whether it helped, instead of guessing. It doesn't need statistics or a paper. It needs one question, one measure, a fair comparison, and honesty about what the result can and can't tell you. This page walks through the steps, gives a one-page plan, six examples for CS TAs, the usual traps, and the ethics, including when classroom data collection needs review by your institution's review board.

Defaults that work. If you only take four things from this page:

  • Change one thing, and keep everything else the same.
  • Measure something you already see or record: counts, minutes, regrade requests, a two-question survey.
  • Get a "before" or a fair comparison. Without one, you can't tell what changed.
  • Report what happened honestly, including "no difference." That is a result too.

The steps

This follows the plan, do, study, act cycle used for testing small changes in many fields.

  1. Notice a problem you see every week. "Office hours turn into me debugging their code."
  2. Ask a question you can answer in two to four weeks, about one change and one outcome. "Does opening with 'What have you tried?' cut the time I spend at the keyboard?"
  3. Pick one measure and a baseline. Collect a week or two of "before" data first, or set up a comparison.
  4. Check the ethics (below), and tell your instructor.
  5. Make the change. Keep everything else the same, and keep a short log of anything unusual.
  6. Look at the results. Compare with the baseline, look at the spread and not only the average, and write down other explanations.
  7. Decide: keep it, adjust it, or drop it. Write one page, including a null result, and share it.

A one-page plan

  • Course, your role, dates.
  • The problem you noticed, in a sentence or two.
  • The one change: exactly what you will do differently.
  • The question: change, then outcome, answerable in four weeks or less.
  • Your prediction: what you expect, and by about how much.
  • The measure, and how you will record it.
  • The comparison: before and after, alternating weeks or sections, or two versions in the same section.
  • How many: students, sessions or submissions.
  • Likely confounds, and how you will reduce them.
  • Ethics check: normal teaching? Everyone gets the benefit? Anonymous? Will you publish? Instructor told?
  • Decision rule: what result means keep, adjust or drop.
  • Results and next step, and who you will share them with.

Common traps

TrapFix
The time of term changes everything (deadline weeks differ)Alternate weeks, or compare the same point in the term
The topic got harder or easierCompare similar assignments or matched questions
Different sections or times of daySwap which section gets the change
You try harder while testingRun it for more than one week, and note your own effort
Students know they're being watchedMeasure things you already record
Different students show upReport attendance; avoid "those who came did better"
Your baseline week was unusually badUse two or more baseline weeks
Survey wording or low responseKeep the same questions, and report the response rate
Small numbersReport raw counts and ranges, and call it a signal, not proof

Reporting honestly

With 20 to 40 students, a difference of a few points can easily be noise. Show the raw numbers, say what else changed during the trial, and describe the result as "worth continuing" rather than "it works." A null or negative result saves you, and the next TA, from a change that doesn't help, so report it the same way. If you want to see what stronger evidence looks like, studies comparing teaching methods across many terms and courses are a good model (two are in the reading list).

Ethics and review

  • Improving your own teaching is usually not "research." Publishing is the line. U.S. federal rules define research as a systematic investigation "designed to develop or contribute to generalizable knowledge." Collecting feedback to improve your own section generally falls outside that.
  • If you might publish, in a conference paper, a poster or a thesis chapter, you need your institution's review board (IRB) approval before you collect any data. It can't be added afterward. Studies of normal educational practice may qualify as exempt, but the IRB decides that, not you.
  • At UMass, the Human Research Protection Office explains when classroom projects need no review, and has a form and a phone line (413-545-3428) for when you're unsure.
  • Don't disadvantage anyone. No group should lose something that affects grades. Swap conditions between sections or weeks, or give everyone the better version afterward.
  • Protect student data. Grades and submissions are education records under FERPA. Keep data in course systems, report only group numbers, never share names or student work, and keep surveys anonymous, optional and separate from grades.
  • Tell your instructor before you start.
